# Finish Powder Shouldn't Exist

This is not an advertisement for Finish Quantum.

In fact, I bought it reluctantly.

I have a small countertop dishwasher that only requires about 8 grams of detergent per load. Buying tablets seemed wasteful when I could buy powder, measure out exactly what I needed, and make a box last much longer.

It was the sensible engineering solution.

Or so I thought.

I tried the recommended 8 grams. I tried more than that. I even tried roughly the amount suggested on the box for a full-sized dishwasher.

The result barely changed.

Every load came out with a chalky residue on the dishes. Glasses looked cloudy. Cutlery felt dusty. Plates never quite looked clean.

The powder was so consistently disappointing that I stopped blaming the detergent.

I blamed the dishwasher.

Convinced the machine itself had somehow become contaminated with detergent residue or grease, I did something I absolutely do **not** recommend.

I ran a cycle with Dawn dish soap.

Yes, really.

Fortunately, I got lucky. My kitchen didn't become a foam party, but it also didn't solve anything. The dishes came out exactly as they always had.

I cleaned the filter. I inspected the spray arms. I questioned everything except the detergent itself.

Eventually Finish Quantum went on sale.

I bought it, mostly because I wanted to eliminate one more variable.

The difference was ridiculous.

Same dishwasher.

Same water.

Same dishes.

Everything came out sparkling clean.

Not "slightly better."

Not "worth paying a little more."

Sparkling.

At that moment I realized this wasn't an article about how good Finish Quantum is.

It's an article about why Finish Powder exists.

## The Problem Isn't the Premium Product

I have no objection to premium products.

If a company develops a better formula that costs more to produce and cleans better, fantastic. Charge more for it.

That's competition.

What bothers me is when the budget product performs so poorly that it stops feeling like a legitimate budget option.

A budget product should still accomplish its basic job.

It shouldn't exist primarily to convince customers to buy the expensive one.

I don't know whether that's what Finish intended.

Maybe the powder genuinely performs well in other dishwashers or under different water conditions. Maybe my dishwasher simply exposes a weakness in the formulation.

But intention doesn't change the customer experience.

My experience wasn't that Quantum impressed me.

It was that Powder made me wonder why it was on the shelf.

## Artificial Product Tiers

This isn't unique to dishwasher detergent.

Software companies hide tiny quality-of-life features behind "Pro" subscriptions.

Cars ship with hardware already installed but disabled until you pay to unlock it.

Printers invent reasons not to print.

Consumer electronics release three nearly identical products where the cheapest one feels artificially constrained.

Again and again, the question seems to become:

"How much better does the next tier need to feel?"

Instead of:

"How good can we make the entry-level product?"

Those are very different philosophies.

One is about building the best product possible at a given price.

The other is about encouraging customers to climb a pricing ladder.

## That's the Part That Bothers Me

Maybe Finish Quantum deserves every bit of praise it gets.

That's not my point.

I'm not recommending Finish Quantum.

I'm criticizing a product strategy that appears across countless industries.

A premium product should justify its higher price by being exceptional.

It shouldn't justify its higher price because the product beneath it leaves customers wondering whether they made a mistake.

Finish Quantum didn't sell me on premium dishwasher detergent.

Finish Powder sold me on the idea that sometimes the real product being engineered isn't the detergent.

It's the pricing ladder.
