I bought Finish Quantum reluctantly, so this should not be mistaken for an advertisement.

I have a small countertop dishwasher that only requires about 8 grams of detergent per load. Buying tablets seemed wasteful when I could buy powder, measure out exactly what I needed, and make a box last much longer.

It seemed like the sensible engineering solution until I used it.

I tried the recommended 8 grams, increased the amount, and eventually used roughly what the box suggested for a full-sized dishwasher. The result barely changed.

Every load came out with a chalky residue on the dishes. Glasses looked cloudy. Cutlery felt dusty. Plates never quite looked clean.

The powder was so consistently disappointing that I stopped blaming it and blamed the dishwasher instead.

Convinced the machine itself had somehow become contaminated with detergent residue or grease, I did something I absolutely do **not** recommend.

I ran a cycle with Dawn dish soap. Yes, really.

Fortunately, I got lucky. My kitchen didn't become a foam party, but it also didn't solve anything. The dishes came out exactly as they always had.

I cleaned the filter, inspected the spray arms, and questioned everything except the detergent itself. When Finish Quantum eventually went on sale, I bought it mostly to eliminate one more variable.

The difference was ridiculous. With the same dishwasher, water, and dishes, everything came out sparkling clean. The improvement was not marginal or merely worth a small premium; it was the result I had expected from detergent in the first place.

At that moment, the interesting question stopped being how good Finish Quantum was and became why Finish Powder existed.

## The Problem Isn't the Premium Product

I have no objection to premium products. If a company develops a better formula that costs more to produce and cleans better, charging more for it is ordinary competition.

What bothers me is when the budget product performs so poorly that it stops feeling like a legitimate budget option.

A budget product should still accomplish its basic job instead of existing primarily to convince customers to buy the expensive one.

I do not know whether Finish intended that effect. The powder may perform well in other machines or water conditions, and my dishwasher may simply expose a weakness in the formulation. Intention does not change the customer experience, though: Quantum impressed me less than Powder made me wonder why it was on the shelf.

## Artificial Product Tiers

Dishwasher detergent is only one instance of the pattern. Software companies hide tiny quality-of-life features behind "Pro" subscriptions, cars ship with installed hardware disabled until the owner pays to unlock it, printers invent reasons not to print, and consumer electronics arrive in nearly identical tiers whose cheapest model feels artificially constrained.

The design question repeatedly shifts from "How good can we make the entry-level product?" to "How much better does the next tier need to feel?" The first philosophy tries to build the best product possible at a given price; the second tries to move customers up a pricing ladder.

## That's the Part That Bothers Me

Finish Quantum may deserve every bit of praise it receives, but recommending it is beside the point. The target here is a product strategy that appears across countless industries.

A premium product should justify its higher price by being exceptional, not because the product beneath it leaves customers wondering whether they made a mistake.

Finish Quantum did not sell me on premium dishwasher detergent. Finish Powder sold me on the idea that, sometimes, the real product being engineered is the pricing ladder.
